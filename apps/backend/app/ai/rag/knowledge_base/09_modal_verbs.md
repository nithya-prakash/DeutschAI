# Modal verbs

Modal verbs express ability, permission, obligation, desire, or possibility, and pair
with a second verb in its **infinitive form**, pushed to the **end of the clause**.
The six core modals:

| Modal | Meaning | ich-form | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| können | can / to be able to | kann | Ich kann schwimmen. (I can swim.) |
| müssen | must / to have to | muss | Ich muss gehen. (I have to go.) |
| dürfen | may / to be allowed to | darf | Ich darf hier parken. (I'm allowed to park here.) |
| wollen | to want to | will | Ich will Deutsch lernen. (I want to learn German.) |
| sollen | should / to be supposed to | soll | Ich soll früh aufstehen. (I'm supposed to get up early.) |
| mögen | to like | mag | Ich mag Kaffee. (I like coffee.) |

Modal verbs are irregular and, unusually, the `ich` and `er/sie/es` forms are
**identical**: "ich kann", "er kann" (not "er kannt").

**Sentence structure** is the key skill here: the modal is the conjugated ("finite")
verb in position two, and the infinitive it governs sits at the very end: "Ich **kann**
morgen nicht **kommen**." (I can't come tomorrow.) Beginners often mistakenly put the
infinitive right after the modal the way English does ("I can come tomorrow not") —
German always sends it to the end of the clause, even when other words come between.

`möchten` (would like to) is technically the subjunctive form of `mögen` but functions
as its own polite modal in everyday speech: "Ich möchte einen Kaffee." (I would like a
coffee.) — more common in requests than the plain `will` (want), which can sound blunt.
