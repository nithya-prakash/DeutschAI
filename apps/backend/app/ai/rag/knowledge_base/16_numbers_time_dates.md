# Numbers, time & dates

**Numbers 0–20**: null, eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn,
elf, zwölf, dreizehn, vierzehn, fünfzehn, sechzehn, siebzehn, achtzehn, neunzehn, zwanzig.
Note `elf` and `zwölf` are irregular; 13–19 follow "digit + zehn" (dreizehn = 3+10).

**21 and up** are built digit-then-ten, joined with `und` (and) — the reverse order of
English: 21 = **ein**und**zwanzig** (literally "one-and-twenty"), 47 =
siebenundvierzig. This reversed order is a common source of number-transposition
mistakes (writing/hearing 47 as 74) and is worth deliberately practicing.

**Telling time** has both a formal (24-hour, used in schedules/announcements) and
casual (12-hour) style:
- Formal: "Es ist vierzehn Uhr dreißig." (It's 14:30.)
- Casual, on the hour: "Es ist drei Uhr." (It's three o'clock.)
- Casual, half hour — this is the trap: "halb drei" means **half past two** (2:30), not
  half past three — German counts the half hour as "halfway to" the next hour, not
  "past" the current one.
- Quarter hours: "Viertel nach drei" (quarter past three), "Viertel vor vier" (quarter
  to four).

**Dates** are written day-month-year, the reverse of US style: 03.10.2026 means 3
October 2026, not March 10th. Ordinal numbers are used for the day and take a period
after the digit: "der dritte Oktober" or written as "der 3. Oktober."

**Asking the time/date**: "Wie spät ist es?" or "Wie viel Uhr ist es?" (What time is
it?); "Welches Datum haben wir heute?" or "Der Wievielte ist heute?" (What's the date
today?).
