# Possessive articles

Possessive articles ("my", "your", "his", etc.) behave like `ein`/`eine` — they take
the same set of endings, so once you know `ein`/`eine`'s pattern, possessives are just
a new stem attached to the same endings:

| Pronoun | Possessive stem | Example (masc./neuter) | Example (fem./plural) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ich | mein- | mein Vater | meine Mutter |
| du | dein- | dein Vater | deine Mutter |
| er/es | sein- | sein Vater | seine Mutter |
| sie (she) | ihr- | ihr Vater | ihre Mutter |
| wir | unser- | unser Vater | unsere Mutter |
| ihr | euer- | euer Vater | eure Mutter |
| sie (they)/Sie | ihr-/Ihr- | ihr Vater / Ihr Vater | ihre Mutter / Ihre Mutter |

Like `ein`, the possessive is unmarked for masculine/neuter in the nominative (mein
Vater, mein Kind) and takes an `-e` for feminine and plural (meine Mutter, meine
Eltern).

**sein vs. ihr** is a common point of confusion because both can mean "his/its" or
"her" depending on who owns the thing: `sein` is used when the *owner* is male or
neuter ("his"/"its"), `ihr` when the owner is female ("her"). The gender of the object
owned is irrelevant to this choice — only the owner's gender matters: "Er hat seine
Tasche" (he has his bag) vs. "Sie hat ihre Tasche" (she has her bag) — `Tasche` is
feminine in both sentences, but the possessive changes based on who the owner is.

Like `ein`, possessives also change endings across the four grammatical cases —
`meinen` in the accusative masculine, for example — following the same pattern
described in the Akkusativ topic.
