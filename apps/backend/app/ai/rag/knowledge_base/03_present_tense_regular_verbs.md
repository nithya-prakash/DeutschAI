# Present tense (regular verbs)

Regular ("weak") German verbs conjugate by removing the `-en` infinitive ending and
adding a personal ending. Take `machen` (to make/do):

| Pronoun | Ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ich | -e | ich mache |
| du | -st | du machst |
| er/sie/es | -t | er macht |
| wir | -en | wir machen |
| ihr | -t | ihr macht |
| sie/Sie | -en | sie machen |

Two spelling adjustments to know:
- If the stem ends in `-t` or `-d` (e.g. `arbeiten`, to work), an extra `-e-` is
  inserted before consonant-starting endings so it stays pronounceable: `du arbeitest`,
  `er arbeitet` (not `arbeitst`/`arbeitt`).
- If the stem ends in a sibilant (`-s`, `-ß`, `-z`, `-x`), the `du`-form drops the
  extra `-s`: `du heißt` (not `heißst`), from `heißen`.

Unlike English, German has only **one** present-tense form where English has three:
"ich mache" covers "I make", "I am making", and "I do make". There is no separate
present-progressive construction in standard German.

The present tense is also regularly used to talk about the **near future**, especially
with a time expression: "Ich mache das morgen" (I'll do that tomorrow) is completely
natural and more common in speech than the grammatical future tense (`werden` + infinitive).
