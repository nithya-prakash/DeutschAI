# Word order (verb-second)

The single most important structural rule in German: in a main clause (a plain
statement), the **conjugated verb is always the second element** — not necessarily the
second *word*, but the second grammatical unit.

"Ich **trinke** heute Kaffee." (I'm drinking coffee today.) — subject first, verb
second. This looks just like English so far. But German allows *any* element to move
to the front for emphasis, and the verb still has to stay second, which forces the
subject to move after it:

"**Heute** **trinke** ich Kaffee." (Today I'm drinking coffee.) — "heute" is now
element one, so the verb "trinke" stays in position two, and the subject "ich" gets
pushed to position three.

This is the opposite of English, where "Today I am drinking coffee" keeps the subject
right before the verb regardless of what's fronted. In German, whatever comes first is
irrelevant to where the verb goes — the verb is *always* second, full stop. This is
why it's called "verb-second" (V2) word order rather than "subject-verb-object."

**Subordinate clauses break this rule**: after conjunctions like `weil` (because),
`dass` (that), `wenn` (if/when), the conjugated verb is pushed all the way to the
**end** of the clause instead: "Ich bleibe zu Hause, **weil** es **regnet**." (I'm
staying home because it's raining.) — compare to the main-clause word order "Es
**regnet**." This verb-final pattern in subordinate clauses is a separate rule from
verb-second in main clauses, and mixing the two up is a very common intermediate-level
mistake.
