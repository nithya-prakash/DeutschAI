# Alphabet & pronunciation

The German alphabet has the same 26 letters as English, plus four extra characters:
ä, ö, ü (umlauts) and ß (eszett, "sharp s"). Getting these right matters — "schon"
(already) and "schön" (beautiful) are different words.

**Umlauts** shift the vowel sound forward in the mouth: ä sounds like the "e" in "bed",
ö is like the "u" in "fur" said with rounded lips, ü is like "ee" said with rounded lips
(similar to French "u"). If a keyboard can't type umlauts, the accepted substitution is
ae, oe, ue, and ss for ß — so "für" can be written "fuer".

**Consonants that differ from English:**
- `w` sounds like English "v" (Wasser = "vasser")
- `v` sounds like English "f" (Vater = "fater")
- `z` sounds like "ts" (Zeit = "tsyte")
- `j` sounds like English "y" (ja = "ya")
- `ch` has two sounds: soft after i/e (ich, "sh"-ish) and hard after a/o/u (ach, like
  Scottish "loch")
- `sch` sounds like English "sh"
- `st` and `sp` at the start of a word sound like "sht" and "shp" (Stadt = "shtat")

**Stress** in German usually falls on the first syllable of a word, especially for
native German words: `WASser`, `SPRECHen`. This is a reliable starting rule even
though compound words and borrowed words have exceptions.
