# sein & haben

`sein` (to be) and `haben` (to have) are the two most common German verbs, and both
are irregular — they must be memorized as whole conjugation tables rather than derived
from a rule.

**sein (to be):**
| ich bin | wir sind |
|---|---|
| du bist | ihr seid |
| er/sie/es ist | sie/Sie sind |

**haben (to have):**
| ich habe | wir haben |
|---|---|
| du hast | ihr habt |
| er/sie/es hat | sie/Sie haben |

Beyond their basic meanings, both verbs are the backbone of other grammar:

- `haben` is the **auxiliary verb** for the Perfekt (past) tense of most verbs:
  "Ich habe gegessen" (I have eaten / I ate).
- `sein` is the auxiliary for verbs of motion or change of state: "Ich bin gegangen"
  (I went), "Ich bin aufgewacht" (I woke up). A useful rule of thumb: if the verb
  describes moving from A to B, or a change from one state to another, it takes `sein`
  in the Perfekt; otherwise it takes `haben`.
- `sein` is also used with age, unlike English: "Ich bin 20 Jahre alt" (I am 20 years
  old) — German never uses `haben` for age the way some other languages use "to have."

Because these two verbs appear in nearly every sentence pattern a beginner needs, it's
worth over-learning their conjugations early — mistakes here are highly noticeable to
native speakers.
