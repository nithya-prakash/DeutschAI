# Negation (nicht/kein)

German splits negation into two words depending on **what** is being negated.

**kein/keine** negates a noun that would otherwise carry `ein`/`eine` or no article at
all — it follows the exact same ein/eine ending pattern: "Ich habe **einen** Hund." →
"Ich habe **keinen** Hund." (I have a dog → I don't have a dog.) "Ich habe Zeit." (I
have time) → "Ich habe **keine** Zeit." (I don't have time.)

**nicht** negates everything else: verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and nouns that already
have a definite article (der/die/das). Its position depends on what it's negating:

- Negating the whole sentence/verb: `nicht` goes at the **end** of the clause. "Ich
  komme heute **nicht**." (I'm not coming today.)
- Negating a specific word (an adjective, adverb, or prepositional phrase): `nicht`
  goes directly **before** that word. "Das ist **nicht** teuer." (That's not
  expensive.) "Ich wohne **nicht** in Berlin." (I don't live in Berlin — as opposed to
  living somewhere else specifically.)
- Negating a noun with a definite article: "Ich sehe **nicht den** Mann, sondern die
  Frau." (I don't see the man, but the woman.)

A reliable test: if you could replace the noun phrase with "a/any" in English, use
`kein`; if the sentence would use "not" anywhere else, use `nicht`. Mixing these up
(saying "nicht ein Hund" instead of "kein Hund") is one of the most common beginner
errors and immediately marks speech as non-native, even though it's usually still
understandable.
