# Imperative

The imperative gives commands or instructions, and — like personal pronouns — has
separate forms depending on who you're addressing.

**du-form**: drop the `-st` ending from the `du`-conjugation (not just the pronoun —
the ending too), and often drop a final `-e` in casual speech: "du kommst" → "**Komm**!"
(Come!). Verbs that change their stem vowel from e→i or e→ie in the present tense keep
that change here: "du sprichst" → "**Sprich**!" (Speak!), "du liest" → "**Lies**!"
(Read!). Verbs whose stem changes a→ä (like fahren) do **not** carry that change into
the imperative: "du fährst" → "**Fahr**!" not "Fähr!"

**ihr-form**: identical to the plain `ihr`-conjugation, just drop the pronoun: "ihr
kommt" → "**Kommt**!" (Come, all of you!)

**Sie-form** (formal, singular or plural): invert to verb-first, keep the pronoun:
"Sie kommen" → "**Kommen Sie**!" (Please come.) This is the most common imperative
form seen in written instructions, signage, and formal requests, since it works for
both one person and a group.

**wir-form** ("Let's..."): also verb-first with the pronoun kept: "**Gehen wir**!"
(Let's go!) — equivalent to English "Let's."

Politeness is usually added with **bitte** (please): "Komm bitte!" or "Bitte kommen
Sie!" — German imperatives without `bitte` can sound blunter than their English
equivalents, especially in the du-form, so adding it is good practice even for simple
requests.
